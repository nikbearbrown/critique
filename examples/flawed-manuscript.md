# Morning Light Exposure Improves Exam Performance in Undergraduates

A. Author, B. Author, C. Author
Department of Psychology, Example University

## Abstract

This study shows that morning light causes better academic performance. Students who sat near windows scored higher on exams than students who did not. Universities should redesign classrooms to maximize daylight. More research is needed.

## Introduction

Light is important for human health. Many studies have looked at light and sleep [1]. Our own earlier work found that light matters for mood [2], alertness [3] and motivation [4]. Academic performance is also important. Nobody has studied whether light in morning lectures affects exam scores. In this paper we study light and exam performance.

## Methods

We recruited 18 undergraduates from one 9 a.m. introductory psychology section. Students chose their own seats on the first day and kept them for the semester. Nine students who sat in the window row formed the light group and nine who sat in the back row formed the control group. Light levels were measured using standard protocols. For six weeks the light group also used desk lamps. We collected exam scores, quiz scores, attendance, self-reported sleep, mood, alertness, caffeine use, study hours, GPA, class participation, note-taking quality, perceived stress, screen time and course satisfaction. Groups were compared with t-tests.

## Results

The light group scored higher on the final exam than the control group (81.2 vs 74.6, p < 0.05), which proves the effect of light. Figure 1 shows the means with error bars. The other measures showed trends in the expected direction and are not reported here. Two students in the control group stopped attending and were removed from the analysis.

## Discussion

Our results prove that morning light improves learning. This is consistent with our earlier findings [2, 3, 4]. The mechanism is circadian entrainment, which increases hippocampal consolidation. Universities should invest in daylight-maximizing classroom redesign, and employers should expect similar productivity gains in offices. A limitation is the sample size. Future work should replicate these results in more students.
